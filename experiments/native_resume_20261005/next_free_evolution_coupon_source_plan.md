# Next PG-Frag coupon gate: source-only design proposal

**State:** design proposal only. No Julia solver was run for this plan and no authorization for a new native run is inferred. The main reviewer chooses the next smallest benchmark and load mode.

## Why the current 125-point case cannot serve as the free-fracture coupon

The frozen point set is a 5 mm cube with 1 mm cell-center spacing and a 3 mm horizon, so `δ/h = 3` while `δ/L = 0.6`. The greatest distance from any point to its nearest external face is only 2.5 mm; therefore every point has a horizon that intersects a free boundary. The native driver constructs `OSBMaterial()`, whose pinned implementation defaults to `NoCorrection`. Those choices are adequate for the narrow threshold branch check, but they do not establish surface response, bulk elastic calibration, crack-path objectivity, or mesh convergence. The B fixture also applied `x' = F X` to every point and called native force/failure evaluation once. It did not integrate an evolving coupon.

The current library exposes `EnergySurfaceCorrection` for OSB as an alternative to the default `NoCorrection`; it must be treated as a model choice to test and freeze, not toggled only to improve a plot. A study of glass-fiber coupons found a three-particle-spacing horizon useful for that study, while dynamic-fracture research separately analyzes horizon, dimension, specimen size, surface effects, and elastic/kinetic/dissipated energy. These papers support sensitivity gates; they do not establish a universal `δ/h` or coupon size for this ceramic-like prototype. [Peridynamics.jl OSB API](https://kaipartmann.github.io/Peridynamics.jl/stable/public_api_reference/#Peridynamics.OSBMaterial), [Mesh Sensitivity in Peridynamic Quasi-static Simulations](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.proeng.2017.02.116), [Peridynamic analysis of dynamic fracture: influence of peridynamic horizon, dimensionality and specimen size](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00466-021-02017-1).

## Proposed gates

1. **Freeze the specimen and time scales from measurements.** Start from a small physical coupon geometry with measured gauge length, width, thickness, density, elastic response, fracture energy, and a defined actuator or impact history. Choose `δ` as a physical model length tied to the process-zone scale and calibration data; choose point spacing `h` separately. Keep a finite interior region whose support is not wholly truncated; a preliminary design heuristic is for the minimum gauge width and thickness to span several horizons (for example, at least `6δ`), then verify specimen-size sensitivity. That number is a candidate for planning, not a universal standard. Use a 3D coupon when the result is meant to support 3D artifact claims.

2. **Separate horizon sensitivity from spatial quadrature convergence.** At a fixed physical `δ`, compare increasing `δ/h` resolutions (for example `3`, `4`, and `5`) with consistent quadrature and record node count, neighbor count, boundary support, stable `Δt`, and force-displacement response. Then vary `δ` separately at a resolution that has passed the `h` study. Freeze one mesh/horizon pair only after global response and crack observables are stable within a declared tolerance. The published three-spacing coupon result is a useful comparator, not a substitute for these checks.

3. **Choose and test the surface model before fracture.** The native prototype currently uses the OSB default `NoCorrection` (`ordinary_state_based.jl:94`). Compare that baseline with the package's OSB `EnergySurfaceCorrection` option using intact elastic free-surface and uniaxial patch checks. Quantify surface displacement/stress, stiffness, and energy behavior. Select one option based on those checks and freeze it in the experiment manifest before any fracture run. Keep the correction choice fixed across mesh/orientation comparisons.

4. **Test objectivity and remeshing as separate gates.** On one fixed point layout, translate and rigidly rotate the body without strain; recomputed bond stretches should remain zero within numerical tolerance, with no damage event and no spurious internal force. This checks kinematic objectivity. Independently rebuild the coupon on rotated or offset point lattices and on refined `h` values while preserving physical dimensions and `δ`; compare force-displacement curves, initiation location/time, and 3D crack surface. This checks discretization/orientation sensitivity. The affine B result does neither test.

5. **Use an explicit physical grip history and let the solver evolve interior points.** For a displacement-controlled tensile coupon, apply time-dependent velocity conditions only to the grip point sets, with bottom restraint and a smooth top/bottom ramp. If the target is a quasi-static preload, define the ramp relative to measured/calibrated longitudinal wave transit time and compare progressively longer ramps; accept quasi-static behavior only when kinetic energy and the force-displacement response converge under longer ramps. If the target is dynamic fracture, prescribe a measured-rate loading or impact waveform instead of relabeling a slow ramp. The native VelocityVerlet step updates half-step velocity, applies boundary conditions, advances `position` and `displacement`, computes native forces/failure, then updates acceleration/velocity (`velocity_verlet.jl:165–183`). The grip history is a real kinematic boundary condition; direct replacement of every point by an affine `F X` before one force call is only a constitutive diagnostic. The public API documents `velocity_bc!(fun(t), ...)` for time-dependent conditions and limits `displacement_bc!` to the NewtonKrylov solver; do not route a dynamic VelocityVerlet case through the unsupported displacement API. [Peridynamics.jl boundary and solver API](https://kaipartmann.github.io/Peridynamics.jl/stable/public_api_reference/).

6. **Build and verify the energy ledger before interpreting a crack.** Record per-step actuator reactions and work, kinetic energy, integrated OSB strain energy, and an independently defined irreversible fracture-energy term; include contact or damping only when they are actually present. First close a reversible, no-failure loading/unloading case. Derive the fracture term from the pinned discrete OSB energy and correction rules, counting reciprocal bond pairs once. Do not infer fracture dissipation from a drop in instantaneous strain energy alone: after failure, native OSB recalculates weighted volume and dilatation over the active graph (`ordinary_state_based.jl:207–234`), and the native pair no longer contributes the same stored energy. Dynamic-fracture work explicitly compares horizon, crack behavior, and elastic/kinetic/dissipated energy, so those quantities should be reported together. [Dynamic-fracture study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00466-021-02017-1).

7. **Run one free-path notched-coupon gate.** Use a geometric notch to create a physical stress concentrator, but leave the remaining bonds intact and failure-enabled. Do not use `precrack!`, manually clear `bond_active`, constrain a damage plane, or set an all-point affine deformation. Let all candidate directions evolve under the native force/failure loop. Capture full-rate point fields and directed/canonical bond histories around initiation, then verify that the transition starts near the notch, obeys the native threshold, remains reciprocal, and does not collapse in one step. Repeat only after the mesh, surface, and energy gates pass; compare crack path and energy under independent mesh orientations.

8. **Hold artifact-scale work behind coupon evidence.** Current `E=5 GPa`, `ν=.24`, `ρ=2100 kg/m³`, and `Gc=45 J/m²` are marked unvalidated prototypes. Do not transfer their crack path or fragment behavior to an actual object. First calibrate against measured coupon data and check convergence in mesh, horizon, surface treatment, load rate, and energy closure. Only then scale to an artifact with measured geometry/material and an experimental comparison.
